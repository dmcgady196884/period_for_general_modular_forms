## 1. The topological / gauge-theoretic picture

### 1.1 The DR-B contours project to generators of $\pi_1$ of the modular curve

The S-contour $[-1/\tau_0, \tau_0]$ and T-contour $[\tau_0-1, \tau_0]$ live in $\mathbb{H}$, but their endpoints are $\Gamma$-equivalent. When you project to the modular curve $Y(1) = \Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}$, each contour becomes a **loop** based at $[\tau_0]$.

These two loops are precisely the generators of the orbifold fundamental group:
$$\pi_1^{\mathrm{orb}}(Y(1)) \;\cong\; \mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z}) \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}/2 * \mathbb{Z}/3,$$
with the two free factors generated by $S$ (order 2) and $ST$ (order 3). Equivalently, $S$ and $T$ generate the full group with relations $S^2 = (ST)^3 = 1$.

**Key point:** the basepoint $\tau_0$ does not affect the homotopy class of these loops. Different $\tau_0$ give different specific paths in $\mathbb{H}$, but the same loop in $Y(1)$ up to homotopy. This is the topological content of "$\tau_0$-gauge-invariance."

### 1.2 The period integral is a holonomy

The polynomial coefficient system $V_{k-2}$ is not a constant vector space over the modular curve — it's a **local system**, a flat bundle whose transition functions are the $\Gamma$-action $(X,Y)\mapsto(aX+bY, cX+dY)$.

Integrating $f(\tau)(X-\tau Y)^{k-2}d\tau$ around the S-loop or T-loop is a **weighted holonomy**: it measures how a vector in the local system rotates as you transport it around the loop, with $f$ providing the weight.

Without the weight $f$, you'd get the bare monodromy matrix — the topological action of $S$ or $T$ on $V_{k-2}$. With the weight $f$, you get something carrying arithmetic information: the periods.

### 1.3 The gauge theory dictionary

This isn't a loose analogy. The mathematical structure is literally that of a Wilson loop:

| Gauge theory | DR-B / modular forms |
|---|---|
| Connection $A_\mu$ on principal bundle | Flat connection on local system $V_{k-2}$ |
| Wilson loop $\mathrm{tr}\,\mathcal{P}\exp\oint_C A$ | $\int_\gamma f(\tau)(X-\tau Y)^{k-2}d\tau$ |
| Gauge transformation by $g$ | Coboundary $\delta P$ |
| Gauge-invariant observable | Cohomology class $[C^f]\in H^1(\Gamma; V_{k-2})$ |
| Choice of trivialization frame | Choice of basepoint $\tau_0$ |
| Trace kills basepoint dependence | Cohomology class kills $\tau_0$ dependence |

The integral around a closed loop returns to where it started up to a $\Gamma$-element acting on $V_{k-2}$ — this is exactly the monodromy of a flat connection, and it's exactly what a Wilson loop computes.

### 1.4 What this buys

A few things, of varying weight:

1. **The §9 analogy in the current draft gains technical teeth.** The gauge-invariance framing isn't just rhetorical; the DR-B period genuinely is a holonomy, in the same precise sense that a Wilson loop is. The note can state this with confidence rather than as a heuristic.

2. **The on-shell amplitude analogy becomes more concrete.** In gauge theory, Wilson loops are gauge-invariant observables that can in principle be computed without ever fixing a gauge — by directly evaluating holonomies. The "manifestly cohomological" construction of $\omega^\pm(f)$ that the §9 open question asks about is, in this language, asking for a holonomy formula that doesn't go through any explicit cocycle representative.

3. **Suggests what kind of object an L-function "is."** For meromorphic forms with interior poles, the relevant analog is a Wilson loop on a punctured manifold, where the punctures introduce additional monodromy data. The wall-crossing residue formula (24) is the analog of having charged defects at the punctures.

## 2. The regularization-as-symptom philosophy

### 2.1 The general thesis

You've been pushing back on the implicit assumption that regularization is a *neutral* tool in analytic number theory — that L-values for non-cuspidal/weakly-holomorphic forms are *necessarily* divergent integrals that must be regulated. The DR-B construction is evidence against that assumption in this specific case: the L-value exists as a manifestly finite holonomy. The BFK regulator, if the equivalence proof closes, is a workaround for the fact that the Mellin contour goes to the cusp where it shouldn't.

In on-shell amplitude language (your historical background): this is the same move as discovering that what looks like a gauge anomaly in the Lagrangian formulation is actually a statement about whether on-shell residues at factorization channels match. The "anomaly" disappears once you're computing the right objects. McGady-Huang 2013 lives in this lineage.

### 2.2 What this might apply to

Some speculation, in decreasing order of plausibility:

**BFK incomplete-gamma regulator** — most plausible target. The conjecture is that this regulator is removable in favor of a manifestly finite finite-cycle integral. Status: subject of the Tier 1 proof. If it closes, this is the demonstration.

**Borcherds-style regularised theta lifts** — speculative. Borcherds regularises divergent integrals over modular curves by analytic continuation in an auxiliary parameter. The structural setup (integrals over non-compact moduli spaces of weakly singular sections) is similar enough that the heuristic "find the right cycle, drop the regulator" might apply. But theta lifts involve non-holomorphic Siegel-Weil kernels, so the DR-B picture doesn't transfer directly. Worth keeping live as a guide for future projects, not a near-term target.

**Rankin-Selberg unfolding for non-cuspidal forms** (Zagier's method) — speculative. The right geometric replacement might exist but it's not obvious what it is.

**Standard analytic continuation of $L(f,s)$** — *not* a regulator in the relevant sense. This is just the functional equation; it doesn't introduce an unphysical scale or break a symmetry.

### 2.3 Honest caveat: sometimes regularization is the real thing

There are cases in physics where regularization genuinely does measure something physical, not paper over a bad framing. Chiral anomalies in even dimensions are the canonical example: the anomaly is a real feature of the quantum theory, it's invariant ('t Hooft anomaly matching), and you cannot make it disappear by being cleverer about contours. The Wess-Zumino consistency condition pins it down cohomologically.

So "regularization as symptom of bad framing" is a strong working heuristic but not a theorem. It's a good prior to bring to a problem, not a guarantee.

The question for the current project: which side does the BFK regulator fall on? The conjecture is that it falls on the "bad framing" side — that there exists a finite computation giving the same answer, and the regulator is dispensable. The Step 3 polar-term asymptotic cancellation is what would settle this.

## 3. Interior poles, the OPE picture, and pole-as-puncture

### 3.1 The OPE interpretation of wall-crossing

Equation (24) of the draft:
$$\delta C^f_S = 2\pi i (2\pi i)^{k-1}\,\mathrm{Res}(f,\tau_p)\,(X - \tau_p Y)^{k-2}$$

This is literally an OPE. The residue $\mathrm{Res}(f,\tau_p)$ is the OPE coefficient — the strength with which the singular operator at $\tau_p$ contributes when the contour passes near it. The polynomial $(X - \tau_p Y)^{k-2}$ is the tensor structure on the auxiliary polarization variables $(X,Y)$ — analogous to how OPE coefficients in tensor theories carry indices.

This isn't a metaphor. The contour-deformation identity in a 2d field theory with operator insertions has exactly this form, with the residue of the integrand being the OPE coefficient. The DR-B integral over a homotopy class of contours, picking up residues when contours brush past insertions, is a literal instance of the standard machinery.

### 3.2 Pole as puncture: the moduli interpretation

A meromorphic $f$ with poles at the $\Gamma$-orbit of $\tau_p\in\mathbb{H}$ is a holomorphic section of a line bundle (with weight) on the *open* modular curve
$$Y(1) \setminus \Gamma\cdot\{\tau_p\}.$$

In moduli language: $Y(1) = \mathcal{M}_{1,1}$, the moduli space of elliptic curves. Removing the $\Gamma$-orbit of $\tau_p$ is the same as marking one additional point on the universal elliptic curve. The natural "moduli home" for meromorphic forms with $n$ pole orbits is something like $\mathcal{M}_{1, n+1}$ (one moving point + $n$ marked-point insertions).

### 3.3 The Brown-Hain connection (where geometry meets the heuristic)

You asked whether the pole/puncture corresponds to a degeneration of a higher-genus surface — like a handle pinching off. The answer is: not directly, but the broader connection you're sensing is real.

The relevant machinery is Brown-Hain's theory of **iterated Eichler integrals** on $\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ and the universal elliptic curve $\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$. The structural fact:

- $\mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ carries a mixed Hodge structure whose periods include modular form periods (genus 1) AND multiple zeta values (genus 0)
- Marked points on the universal elliptic curve enrich this period theory, mixing in additional genus-0 polylogarithmic data
- Interior poles in modular forms are equivalent to marked-point insertions in this sense

So your intuition that "poles connect to higher-genus structure" is in the right ballpark, but the cleaner statement is: **poles enrich the period theory by mixing genus-1 modular data with genus-0 multiple-zeta data via the universal elliptic curve.**

This is the deeper context for the interior-pole case of the L-function story. The wall-crossing formula (24) almost certainly fits into Brown's broader framework as a special case of something known. Locating it explicitly is a worthwhile diligence step before claiming novelty for any interior-pole results.

References worth reading with this question in mind:
- Brown, "A class of non-holomorphic modular forms III" (~2019)
- Hain, "Notes on the universal elliptic KZB equation" (arXiv:1309.0580)
- Enriquez, "Analogues elliptiques des nombres multizétas" (2014)

### 3.4 Quantized anomalies — set aside

A passing thought during chat was whether the wall-crossing residue could be interpreted as a quantized anomaly (in the physics sense, where anomalies satisfy cohomological consistency conditions and live in characteristic classes). On reflection: residues are continuous data taking complex values, with no integrality or quantization. The Wess-Zumino structure that pins down quantized anomalies isn't present here. The right framing is "monodromy/holonomy on a punctured curve," not "quantized gauge anomaly." This heuristic is set aside.

(Exception worth flagging: for *arithmetic* meromorphic modular forms — those with poles at CM points and residues in number fields — the residue data does acquire arithmetic structure, and this connects to Damerell-style special-value theorems via the motivic Galois theory of periods. But that's an arithmetic phenomenon, not a quantization phenomenon. Different beast.)

## 4. Methodological observations

### 4.1 The on-shell methods analogy is genuinely structural, not just rhetorical

In gauge theory amplitudes, the on-shell programme (BCFW, unitarity cuts, helicity formalism) works by:
1. Building amplitudes directly from gauge-invariant on-shell building blocks
2. Never introducing gauge-fixing or off-shell propagators
3. Computing observables without ever encountering the gauge artifacts that look fundamental from the Lagrangian side

The translation to the DR-B story:
1. Build periods directly from cohomologically-meaningful (cuspidal cohomology) objects
2. Never introduce a basepoint or cocycle representative
3. Compute $\omega^\pm(f)$ from $\{a_f(n)\}$ without going through $C^f$ or $\delta P$

The §9 open question in the draft is asking for the modular-form analog of this programme. The BFK exponentially-convergent $q$-series is a partial step in this direction (input is Fourier coefficients, output is periods, no $C^f$ or $P$ in the final formula), but the kernels in that series were derived via $\delta_T^{-1}$, so the cocycle scaffolding is implicit. A truly "on-shell" construction would derive the kernels independently — perhaps via an independent characterisation of $H^1_{\mathrm{cusp}}(\Gamma; V_n)$ coordinates.

This may not be tractable, but the analogy gives the question a precise shape.

### 4.2 The instinct to skip regularization is consistent with your training

Your historical work on anomalies-in-gauge-theory-via-on-shell-S-matrix is exactly the same methodological move as this project: find a frame where the unphysical intermediate scaffolding doesn't appear, and the calculation proceeds directly between physical observables. McGady-Huang and the broader Arkani-Hamed amplituhedron lineage operationalise this for amplitudes; the DR-B framework operationalises it for modular form periods.

This is worth flagging because it's a coherent through-line in your work, not a random hobby tangent. The methodological instinct that drove your PhD work is the same one that's driving this. That's a feature, not a coincidence — and it means you have unusual taste calibration for what counts as a "natural" formulation in this kind of problem.

## 5. Project framing and timing

### 5.1 What's plausibly the right immediate scope

The BFK = DR-B equivalence for $\hat\Delta$, via the Step 1–2–3 strategy, is on its own a paper-worthy result if it closes. The framing is clean: "L-values of weakly holomorphic forms are manifestly finite holonomies on the modular curve, not regulated divergent integrals." That has a clear story arc, is technically self-contained, and doesn't require any of the harder interior-pole machinery.

This is the "small paper" version. Maybe 12–18 pages including the topological/holonomy exposition. It doesn't require committing to anything bigger.

### 5.2 What's plausibly the larger project

The interior-pole case is the natural sequel and is genuinely new territory. The combination of (a) the DR-B finite-cycle picture extended to meromorphic forms, (b) closed-form L-function expressions with explicit residue contributions, (c) the Brown-Hain context — is potentially a substantial paper, possibly several papers.

The wall-crossing identity (24) almost certainly fits into Brown's iterated-Eichler-integral framework as a known special case. The literature check on this is essential before claiming the formulation as new. If Brown has it implicitly: your contribution is making the finite-cycle picture explicit and computational, which is still worthwhile but the framing changes. If Brown does not have it: there's a clean paper just stating and proving the meromorphic generalization.

### 5.3 The hobby pace

The decades-as-hobby framing is, on reflection, well-matched to the larger project rather than the small one. The small paper is closeable in a focused burst (months, maybe a year of weekends). The bigger story rewards slow accumulation: reading Brown carefully, working through KZB, getting fluent in the universal elliptic curve picture. That's the kind of project where intermittent depth over years compounds.

Both paths are available. Neither requires the other to happen first. The small paper is a low-risk way to put a clean technical statement on record while the bigger project marinates.
